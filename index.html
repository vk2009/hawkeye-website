02 — Motivation

Why This Project?

Driving Question

"Can a single consumer-grade camera replicate the ball-tracking accuracy of professional DRS systems, and make fair decision review accessible for amateur cricket?"

I have played cricket for as long as I can remember. Whether it was a local club match or a competitive league game, the sport has always been more than just a pastime — it is a community, a discipline, and a source of some of my most vivid memories. But cricket at the amateur level carries a frustration that professionals rarely have to endure: bad umpire decisions that stick.

At professional level, the Decision Review System (DRS) exists precisely to solve this problem. Hawkeye cameras, ball-tracking algorithms, and instant replays give both teams a mechanism to challenge incorrect calls. A clear LBW that was given not-out can be overturned in seconds. But for the hundreds of thousands of players competing in club leagues and school tournaments around the world, none of that technology exists. A raised finger is final, no matter how wrong it is.

I experienced this personally. In a crucial league match, a clearly missing delivery was given LBW against our best batter, and it cost us the game. There was no recourse, no review, no way to even see what the ball was doing. That moment stuck with me — not out of bitterness, but because it felt solvable. The algorithms behind DRS are not secret. Computer vision has become accessible. Cameras are cheap.

🏏

The Problem

Amateur leagues have no access to ball-tracking DRS. Incorrect LBW and catch decisions go unchallenged, unfairly altering match outcomes.

💡

The Need

A low-cost, single-camera solution that can be set up at any ground — no broadcast rig, no expensive hardware, no specialist operator required.

🎯

The Goal

Reconstruct 3D ball trajectory from 2D footage, predict impact and pitch points, and render a visual review — accessible to every club.

This project sits at the intersection of two things I care deeply about: cricket and computer science. Building Hawkeye DRS from scratch — from the physics engine to the 3D visualisation — felt like the right way to spend a year of serious work, because the outcome would be something genuinely useful to the sport that shaped me.

03 — Process

How I Built It

The project unfolded across several distinct phases — each one revealing new challenges that required a step back, more research, and a revised approach. What started as a computer vision task quickly grew into a full pipeline spanning physics simulation, 3D rendering, and real-time tracking.

1

Research & Scoping

I started by studying published DRS methodology and academic papers on monocular ball tracking. The key challenge with a single camera is depth — without a second viewpoint, you cannot directly observe the Z-axis. I identified that trajectory extrapolation using known physics (gravity, seam movement) could substitute for a second camera in many scenarios. I scoped the project to focus on LBW review: pitch point, impact point, and wicket prediction.

2

Camera Input & Ball Detection

I wrote camera_input.py to handle video ingestion and frame processing. Using OpenCV, I implemented background subtraction and circular Hough transforms to detect the ball in each frame. The detection pipeline had to be robust against pitch markings, fielders, and motion blur at typical amateur video frame rates (30–60 fps).

3

Trajectory Engine

The trajectory_engine.py module takes the 2D pixel coordinates detected across frames and fits a physical trajectory using least-squares regression against a projectile model. Depth is inferred from the apparent size change of the ball as it travels down the pitch. This gave me a reconstructed 3D path that could be extrapolated forward to predict where the ball would have continued had it not hit the pad or bat.

4

3D Visualisation in Blender

With trajectory data in hand, I built a full cricket pitch scene in Blender and wrote blender.py to programmatically animate a ball object along the computed path. This script runs inside Blender's Python environment, reading trajectory output and driving the ball's keyframes. I iterated through multiple versions (hawkeye2.blend, hawkeye3.blend) to improve camera angle, lighting, and the clarity of the impact and prediction zones.

5

Rendering & Output

The final pipeline renders a video showing the ball's actual tracked path (solid line) and predicted continuation (dashed line), with visual markers at the pitch point, impact point, and projected wicket contact. I rendered multiple simulation variants to validate the visualisation against known ball paths, checking that pitch point and impact markers were accurate to within acceptable margins.

6

Testing & Iteration

I tested the system against recorded video of live deliveries, comparing my system's predicted trajectory against the naked-eye observation of where the ball actually went. I tuned the depth-inference model and the trajectory extrapolation window through several rounds of iteration, significantly improving accuracy for balls pitching on a good length.

# trajectory_engine.py — core trajectory reconstruction def fit_trajectory(pixel_coords, frame_timestamps): # Build observation matrix: x(t), y(t) from detections t = np.array(frame_timestamps) x = np.array([p[0] for p in pixel_coords]) y = np.array([p[1] for p in pixel_coords]) # Fit parabolic model to y(t): y = a*t² + b*t + c (gravity) coeffs_y = np.polyfit(t, y, 2) coeffs_x = np.polyfit(t, x, 1) # linear in x return coeffs_x, coeffs_y def predict_wicket_impact(coeffs_x, coeffs_y, stumps_x_pixel): # Extrapolate forward to stumps plane t_impact = (stumps_x_pixel - coeffs_x[1]) / coeffs_x[0] y_impact = np.polyval(coeffs_y, t_impact) return t_impact, y_impact

Throughout the process I kept a build log and iteratively rendered test simulations. The renders in the Image Renders folder represent key checkpoint moments where I verified the visual output matched the expected trajectory.

04 — Product

The Final Product

The product is a fully functional ball-tracking and trajectory-visualisation pipeline that takes a single video feed of a cricket delivery and produces a professional-style DRS review output — the kind you see on broadcast TV, built to run on consumer hardware.

The system is composed of three integrated components: a detection module that locates the ball in every frame of the video, a trajectory engine that reconstructs the 3D path from 2D observations using physics-based inference, and a Blender rendering pipeline that produces a clear, animated visualisation of the ball's actual path alongside its predicted continuation.

The output video shows the ball travelling from release to impact, with a pitch-point marker appearing where the ball first makes contact with the ground, an impact marker at the point it struck the batter's pad or bat, and — critically — a predicted trajectory line showing where the ball would have continued had it not been interrupted. This predicted path is compared against the wicket geometry to determine whether the delivery was hitting the stumps: the core of every LBW decision.

The entire system runs from a single camera positioned side-on or from behind the stumps — the standard angles available at any amateur ground. No specialist hardware is required beyond a video camera or smartphone capable of recording at 30fps or higher. The pipeline is open-source and designed to be run from a laptop at the ground's edge.
